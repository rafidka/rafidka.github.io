Brain Fog Self-Check

A structured, research-informed look at your thinking over the past two weeks. It takes about 8–10 minutes.

Before you start

This is a screening aid, not a diagnosis. “Brain fog” is not a medical diagnosis. It is a loose label for problems with attention, memory, mental speed and mental fatigue, and it turns up in more than a dozen conditions. This questionnaire has not been clinically validated. Its thresholds are practical rules of thumb, not established cutoffs.

Also keep in mind that how foggy people feel matches their scores on objective cognitive tests only weakly. Mood, stress and sleep often explain more of the feeling. A high score here shows that you are bothered by these symptoms and that they affect your life. It does not tell you why.

Privacy: everything runs in your browser. Your answers are never sent anywhere. They are saved only on this device so you don’t lose your progress. At the end you can copy a text summary and share it if you want to.

Stop and get urgent help (call emergency services) if you have sudden:

  • confusion, disorientation, or trouble speaking or understanding speech
  • weakness, numbness or drooping on one side of the face or body
  • loss of vision, double vision, or sudden loss of balance
  • a severe headache unlike any you have had before, or a seizure
These can be signs of a stroke or another emergency. Get help even if the symptoms have gone away.

How this questionnaire was built (and its limits)

What it measures

Part 1 covers six areas of thinking that research on brain fog keeps coming back to: attention, working memory, memory and word-finding, mental speed and clarity, planning and organising (executive function), and mental fatigue. These are the same areas covered by established self-report measures such as PROMIS Cognitive Function and the Cognitive Failures Questionnaire. Parts 2–6 separate how much the symptoms bother you from what might be causing them.

Changes from the earlier draft

  • How often vs. how you’ve changed. The earlier items asked both at once (e.g. “more than it used to” answered on a never–always scale). Now each item asks only how often something happens, and each area gets its own “compared with your usual self” question. This replaces the earlier asterisk marks.
  • One idea per question. Items that bundled several questions into one (for example understanding, deciding and responding) were split or simplified.
  • Clearer answer options. Each option now says roughly how often it means (e.g. “most days”), so “often” means the same thing to everyone.
  • A separate scale for effects on daily life. “How much” replaces “how often” here, and “other people noticed” became a yes/no question.
  • Mood is now screened. Depression and anxiety are strongly linked to feeling foggy, so the public-domain PHQ-4 is included and taken into account in the results.
  • Specific checks for sleep, sleep apnoea and post-exertional malaise replaced single catch-all questions.
  • A clear duration question, and a note in the results if the pattern looks lifelong rather than new.

Known limits

  • No clinical validation. Thresholds (area average ≥ 2.0, i.e. “sometimes” or more often) are rules of thumb. There are no population norms, so comparing with friends tells you about your friends, not about what’s “normal”.
  • Answering from memory about two weeks is affected by your current mood. For a more reliable picture, rate clarity, concentration, memory, speed and mental fatigue (0–4) every evening for a week, and note your sleep.
  • Validated tools exist but are licensed and can’t be reproduced here. PROMIS Cognitive Function gives standardised T-scores, and a clinician can give it to you. The Brain Fog Scale (Debowska et al., 2024) is copyrighted and has no validated cutoffs yet.
